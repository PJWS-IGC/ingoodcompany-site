The Grieving Brain Is Not Failing. It Is Searching.

Grief Counsellors / Therapists Complicated grief Professional support Trauma-informed care Death literacy July 26, 2026

If you have sat across from someone eighteen months after a death and heard them describe their person in the present tense, you have already met the thing this research measured. A 2026 review in Trends in Neurosciences found that in people with prolonged grief disorder, the brain regions handling reward and anticipation stay switched on. The orbitofrontal cortex, the striatum, the nucleus accumbens. They keep producing the signal that the person is still expected. Mary-Frances O'Connor, who wrote The Grieving Brain, puts it plainly: someone with severe grief still shows a reward response to a photograph. The neural equivalent of expecting them to walk back through the door.

That reframes something many of us have quietly worried about in our own assessments. When someone cannot move forward, we have been trained to look for avoidance, for unfinished business, for a person who resists acceptance. The neurobiology points somewhere less accusatory. The attachment system is doing the exact job it was built to do. It is searching for the person it bonded to, and it has not received the update.

The clinical consequence matters more than the science does. Prolonged grief disorder is distinct from bereavement related depression and anxiety, and it does not respond well to antidepressants or to interpersonal psychotherapy. Prolonged Grief Therapy, a sixteen session protocol built around specific healing milestones, brings improvement in roughly seventy percent of patients. Getting the diagnosis right is not academic housekeeping. It decides whether someone spends four years in treatment that was never going to work.

Scale it for this country. Statistics Canada recorded 326,779 deaths in 2024. An estimated four percent of bereaved people develop prolonged grief disorder, and every death leaves more than one person bereaved. That is a caseload, not an edge case. MyGrief.ca from Canadian Virtual Hospice is free and it is good, but it is self directed education, not treatment, and the distinction is one families cannot be expected to make on their own.

When you meet grief that will not soften, what would change in your assessment if you started from the assumption that the brain is searching rather than resisting?

Reading this from another corner of the field?

For hospice, palliative, and healthcare teams and doulas who meet families first, the reframe from stuck to searching changes assessment and referral, and antidepressants are not the answer: Prolonged Grief Therapy is.

Source: National Geographic, Why some grief never fades (March 24, 2026); Trends in Neurosciences, neurobiological review of prolonged grief disorder (2026); Statistics Canada, Deaths 2024

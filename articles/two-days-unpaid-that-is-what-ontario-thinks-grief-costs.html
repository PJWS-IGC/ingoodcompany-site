Two Days. Unpaid. That Is What Ontario Thinks Grief Costs.

Social Workers Professional support Caregiver support Death literacy Difficult conversations July 26, 2026

MyGriefAngels launched a Grief at Work Insights hub at the end of April. It is free, it needs no registration, and it tracks bereavement leave law and policy reform across jurisdictions worldwide alongside research on workplace reintegration. It is a good resource and it is worth bookmarking. It is also, read a certain way, a fairly damning artifact. Somebody had to build a global tracker because the entitlements are so inconsistent that no one can hold them in their head.

Here is the Canadian picture, since that is the one that matters for the people we serve. In Ontario, an employee gets up to two unpaid days per calendar year after two weeks of employment. Not two days per death. Two days per year. In Alberta, three unpaid days after ninety days of employment, again per calendar year. In British Columbia, up to three unpaid days on the death of an immediate family member. In Quebec, five days with the first two paid. If you happen to work in a federally regulated industry, ten days with the first three paid. Same country. Same grief.

Set that beside what we know about how grief actually behaves. Prolonged grief disorder is not diagnosed until symptoms persist past twelve months. Acute grief routinely disrupts sleep, concentration, and decision making for far longer than a long weekend. The statutory minimum in most of this country expires before the funeral flowers do.

The gap between two unpaid days and twelve months is not a policy detail. It is the reason so many people arrive in our offices having already been back at a desk for months, performing competence, quietly falling apart. Every one of us in this work has met that person. Most of us have been that person.

What would you tell an employer who asked you honestly how much time a grieving employee actually needs?

Reading this from another corner of the field?

For counsellors, hospice and doula practitioners, the structural fact is the useful one: clients who went back to work in three days and fell apart at four months did not fail, they hit a statutory floor, and naming that reframes their guilt as a policy problem.

The therapy worked. The part worth noticing is where it can reach.

Grief Counsellors / Therapists Complicated grief AI & technology in grief Professional support Trauma-informed care August 5, 2026

A study published in the European Journal of Psychotraumatology this spring tested a fully remote treatment for prolonged grief disorder, called internet cognitive therapy for prolonged grief. Eight adults who met the diagnostic criteria completed a twelve-session programme over fourteen weeks, delivered online with a therapist supporting them through weekly calls and messages. Seven of the eight showed reliable improvement, alongside drops in the depression, anxiety and post-traumatic symptoms that so often travel with complicated grief. It is a small developmental case series, not a large trial, and it should be read as promising rather than settled.

For clinicians, the headline is not that grief therapy works. We know structured grief therapy works. The headline is that a properly structured version held up when it was delivered entirely at a distance, to people who never sat in the room. That distinction is the whole story for anyone practising outside a city.

Consider what access actually looks like in this country. In 2023 the median wait from a family doctor to a specialist ran close to twenty-eight weeks. Roughly half a million rural Ontarians do not have a family doctor to start that clock. Grief counselling with real training in prolonged grief is concentrated in urban centres, and the person in a northern town who is eight months into a grief that has stopped moving is often told the nearest help is three hours away or a season-long waitlist. Remote treatment that keeps its clinical structure is not a convenience for that person. It is the difference between care and no care.

The caution matters as much as the promise. Remote delivery only helps if it stays actual therapy, with a trained clinician and a real protocol, and does not quietly become an app and a worksheet marketed as the same thing. Canadian resources like MyGrief.ca do valuable work, but they are education, not treatment, and the gap between the two is exactly where families get lost. The opportunity in this research is to close distance without lowering the standard of care.

What would it take for evidence-based grief treatment to reach the rural client as reliably as it reaches the urban one?

Reading this from another corner of the field?

For hospice and palliative teams, social workers, and rural clinicians making referrals, the finding is that structured grief therapy can hold its shape at a distance, so long as it stays real treatment and not an app.

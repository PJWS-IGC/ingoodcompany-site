Ontario Has a Death Doula Network. Most Families Will Never Hear About It in Time.

Death Doulas Death at home Death literacy Professional support July 15, 2026

The Death Doula Ontario Network was founded in 2020 by a registered nurse who saw something the system had not caught up to. People were dying without anyone whose only job was to be present for the dying and the family around them. Not to treat, not to manage a facility, not to run a service afterward. Just to accompany. Five years on, the network exists, the practitioners are trained and working, and most Ontario families still have no idea the role is available to them.

The demand is not theoretical. Nearly one in four Canadians will be over sixty-five by 2030. More people will die at home, or wish to, than our formal systems are built to support. Death doulas fill a gap that hospice waitlists and overstretched palliative teams cannot, and they do it in the space between the medical and the ceremonial, the exact space many of us in this community already occupy.

There is a harder truth underneath the growth. Death doula work in Canada remains entirely unregulated and is not covered by any insurance, public or private. That means access tracks with who can pay out of pocket, which is the same inequity that shapes so much of end-of-life care. A profession this valuable sitting entirely outside the funded system is not a footnote. It is a policy question this field should be asking out loud.

If a role this needed is growing entirely outside the funded system, who gets left out, and what do we owe them?

Reading this from another corner of the field?

For funeral homes, hospice and palliative teams, and clinicians discharging patients home, doulas are an upstream partner rather than a competitor: knowing a trusted local doula lets you serve families across the whole arc of a death, and naming the affordability gap is part of that.

The Lancet Just Said Out Loud What This Field Has Known for Years

Grief Counsellors / Therapists Complicated grief Professional support Death literacy July 15, 2026

The Lancet published a definitive review of prolonged grief disorder this year, and for those of us who work close to the bereaved, the news is not the findings. It is who is saying them. When one of the most authoritative medical journals in the world confirms that roughly one in ten bereaved people develop a grief that does not move, that it has its own neurobiology separate from depression and PTSD, and that it responds to specific therapy at close to a seventy percent improvement rate, a threshold has been crossed. Grief is now a clinical matter the medical mainstream can no longer file under time heals.

For the people in this community, that recognition lands two ways. If you are a grief counsellor or therapist, it is validation of what your caseload already told you, and it is leverage. Evidence-based recognition is how a condition earns funding, referral pathways, and a place in training. If you are a funeral director, a celebrant, a hospice worker, or a death doula, you are often the first person to see a family in the rawest hours of loss, long before any clinician does. You are not being asked to diagnose. You are being asked to notice, and to know where to send someone.

In Canada the pathway exists, though most families never find it on their own. Health Canada recognizes prolonged grief disorder. CAMH runs bereavement support groups and connects people to grief therapists. Hospice Palliative Care Ontario maintains grief and bereavement supports across the province, and free resources like MyGrief.ca and Bereaved Families of Ontario are there for anyone. A Canadian palliative study found five percent of bereaved caregivers met the full criteria and another fourteen percent sat just below it. That is one in five carrying more than ordinary grief, in one program alone.

If the medical world now treats prolonged grief as a condition with a treatment, what is our responsibility to know the signs before a family reaches the clinician's door?

Reading this from another corner of the field?

For hospice teams, doulas, and funeral directors who meet families first, this is the case for structured bereavement follow-up and for knowing the referral pathway, since you are often the early-warning layer long before a clinician is.

Source: The Lancet, Prolonged Grief Disorder Review (2025/2026); Canadian context: CAMH, Hospice Palliative Care Ontario, MyGrief.ca, Bereaved Families of Ontario

Prolonged Grief Disorder Is Now a Diagnosis. In Canada, the Treatment Behind It Is Hard to Find.

Grief Counsellors / Therapists Complicated grief Professional support Trauma-informed care Death literacy August 2, 2026

Since March 2022, prolonged grief disorder has had a place in the DSM-5-TR. The criteria are specific. Grief that stays intense and disabling more than a year after a death, in a way that disrupts a person's ability to function. Naming it matters, because it tells a grieving person that what they are living is real and recognized, not a failure of will.

The harder truth for those of us who do this work is what sits behind the diagnosis. The treatment with the strongest evidence, prolonged grief disorder therapy, is a sixteen-session structured approach developed at Columbia, formerly known as complicated grief therapy. In trials it produced a response rate around 71 percent, more than double the rate seen for standard depression treatment in the same patients. It works. The problem is finding someone trained to deliver it.

In Canada that is a real gap. PGDT training is concentrated in the United States, the clinician directory is maintained there, and few Canadian practitioners have completed it. A family in rural Ontario or the North with a clear prolonged grief presentation is far more likely to reach general grief counselling, a waitlist, or a self-directed tool than a trained PGDT clinician. The Canadian Virtual Hospice built MyGrief.ca, launched in 2016 and free to use, precisely because so many people cannot reach in-person grief support at all. It is good, and it is not treatment. It is education holding the space where a clinical service should be.

So we have arrived somewhere strange. We can now name prolonged grief disorder with precision, and we cannot reliably treat it. For grief counsellors that is not a reason for despair. It is a reason to push for training access on this side of the border, and to be honest with clients about what the system can and cannot yet offer them.

Now that we can diagnose prolonged grief disorder, what do we owe the people we name it in, if the treatment for it is still an ocean away?

Reading this from another corner of the field?

For hospice bereavement programs, family doctors, and social workers, the practical role is early screening and honest navigation: name the diagnosis, know that PGDT is the evidence-based treatment, and be candid that Canadian access is thin, especially rurally.

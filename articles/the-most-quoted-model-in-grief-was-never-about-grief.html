The most quoted model in grief was never about grief.

Community Educators Death literacy Complicated grief Professional support Family communication August 5, 2026

If you do this work, you have met the person who thinks they are failing at it. They tell you they should be at acceptance by now. They read somewhere that anger comes before bargaining, and they are stuck on anger eight months in, and they have decided that means something is wrong with them. Simply Psychology pulled the current research together this year, and it says plainly what many practitioners have been saying quietly for decades. The five stages were never built for the bereaved.

Elisabeth Kubler-Ross drew denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance from interviews with more than two hundred dying patients in the 1960s. She was describing how some people face their own death, not how anyone mourns someone else. The model was never based on systematic study of grief, and it was never meant to be a sequence. Kubler-Ross said so herself in her later work, that the stages were not stops on a timeline and that not everyone moves through all of them or in any order. The ladder was added by other people, and then it was handed to grieving families as if it were a set of instructions.

That matters to anyone who teaches, counsels, or simply sits with grieving people, because the model does real harm when it is misused. A person who believes grief is a five-step process they are meant to complete will read their own non-linear, circling, years-long grief as a personal failure. The most useful sentence a practitioner can offer is not a better map. It is permission. Grief is individual, it does not resolve on a schedule, and moving through it is not the same as finishing it.

The people we serve did not invent the pressure they feel. They absorbed it from a culture that turned an observation about dying into a rulebook for mourning. We can hand some of that back.

What is the sentence you use to give a grieving person permission to stop measuring themselves?

Reading this from another corner of the field?

If you counsel or sit with the grieving, this is a permission slip you can hand over directly: the stages were drawn from dying patients, not the bereaved, and no one is grieving on the wrong timeline.

Source: Simply Psychology, Beyond the Five Stages of Grief: What Modern Research Actually Tells Us (2026); Frontiers in Psychology, Stages of Grief Portrayed on the Internet (2021)

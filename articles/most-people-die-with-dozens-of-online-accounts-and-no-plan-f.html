Most people die with dozens of online accounts and no plan for any of them. In Ontario, the law has not caught up either.

Estate Lawyers / Planners End-of-life planning Death literacy Difficult conversations AI & technology in grief July 22, 2026

The average adult now holds well over a hundred online accounts, and most have made no arrangement for what happens to them after death. For estate planners and the celebrants, funeral directors, and families who sit downstream of that gap, this is no longer a novelty problem. It is a routine one. Photos, financial logins, loyalty points, business domains, and years of correspondence all sit behind passwords that die with the person.

Canada has a framework for this. The Uniform Law Conference of Canada adopted the Uniform Access to Digital Assets by Fiduciaries Act back in 2016, giving executors and attorneys a clear default right to access a person's digital property. The catch is that a uniform act only matters where a province enacts it. Saskatchewan did, in 2020. Prince Edward Island, New Brunswick, and Yukon followed. Ontario has not. That leaves executors here relying on a patchwork of platform policies and terms of service written by companies in another country.

For practitioners, the useful move is to name this in the planning conversation rather than at the graveside. A will can grant a fiduciary authority over digital assets even where provincial law is silent, and a simple inventory of accounts and access wishes spares a grieving family months of locked doors. This is not a technology conversation. It is an estate conversation that happens to involve technology.

When you help someone plan, do you ask what they want done with their digital life, or only their physical one?

Reading this from another corner of the field?

For doulas, funeral directors, and anyone helping with advance care planning, the move is to add a simple inventory of accounts and access wishes to the planning checklist, since incapacity, not only death, triggers the locked-account problem.

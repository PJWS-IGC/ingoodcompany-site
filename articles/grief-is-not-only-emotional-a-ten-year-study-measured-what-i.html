Grief Is Not Only Emotional. A Ten-Year Study Measured What It Costs the Body.

Healthcare Professionals Complicated grief Professional support Caregiver support Trauma-informed care August 2, 2026

If you have worked in health care long enough, you have watched a patient's spouse decline in the months after a death and wondered whether the two were connected. A study out of Denmark, published in Frontiers in Public Health, followed 1,735 bereaved people for a decade and put a number on what you suspected. The people whose grief stayed persistently high were 88 percent more likely to die within ten years than those whose grief stayed low.

The mortality figure is not the part that should change your practice. This is. The highest-risk group, about six percent of the cohort, was identifiable before the loss. They were already being prescribed antidepressants and sedatives more often than their peers while their loved one was still alive. The vulnerability was sitting in the record. What was missing was a system built to act on it.

The lead researcher, Dr. Mette Kjaergaard Nielsen, described the intervention in plain terms. A family doctor could look for prior signs of depression, book a bereavement follow-up appointment, and refer where needed. It is simple, and it is almost never built into how we send a family home after a death.

In Canada the gap is not theoretical. Statistics Canada recorded 326,779 deaths in 2024, and every one of them left people behind. The Canadian Grief Alliance ran the largest grief survey this country has seen, nearly 3,900 respondents from every province and territory, and half of grieving Canadians said they did not feel adequately supported. The Danish study tells us who is most at risk. The Canadian numbers tell us we are not reaching them.

If the record already shows who is most at risk before the loss, whose job is it to make the follow-up call, and when did we decide it was no one's?

Reading this from another corner of the field?

For hospice and palliative teams, social workers, and funeral directors, the pre-loss identification finding is the actionable one: the highest-risk survivor is often visible in the record before the death, and a bereavement follow-up call is the intervention.

Source: Neuroscience News (July 25, 2025), reporting on Nielsen et al., Grief trajectories and long-term health effects in bereaved relatives, Frontiers in Public Health (2025); Canadian Grief Alliance national grief survey; Statistics Canada deaths, 2024
